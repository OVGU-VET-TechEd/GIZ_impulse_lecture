Good morning, everyone, and welcome. I am Hannes Tegelbeckers from Otto von Guericke University Magdeburg, where I work on how people learn technical work. In the next fifteen minutes I want to share a practical view: what should we, the TVET and labour market community, do with AI – and what can we leave to others?
Setting the scene · ~2 min

AI is already here. The question is how we use it.

Let us start with something we all share. AI is already part of our working and learning lives. For us, the question is not how to build AI – it is how to use it well for real problems.

AI arrives quietly

  • Spell-check and translation on your phone
  • Voice messages turned into text
  • Chatbots answering customer questions
  • Route planning for delivery drivers
  • CVs sorted by recruitment platforms

AI, in plain words: software that has learned patterns from many examples – and uses them to suggest, predict, sort or write.

It does not understand like a person. It can be confidently wrong.

Think about your week so far. Most of you have used AI already – probably without calling it AI. [Click] Imagine Amina, a young job-seeker. Her phone corrects her spelling and turns her voice messages into text. And when she applies online, a platform may sort her CV before any person reads it. [Click] That is the definition I will use today. And please keep the second line in mind: AI can sound very sure and still be wrong.

Apply, don't build

Building big AI models costs huge amounts of money, data and energy – and only a handful of companies do it.

Our strength is applying existing tools to real problems:
matching people to jobsmaking training more relevantsupporting teachers
We do not need to build the engine – we need to train good drivers and build good roads.
Here is the frame for the whole talk. Building large AI models from scratch is extremely expensive and is done by a handful of companies. That is not our job – and that is good news. [Click] Our strength lies somewhere else: using tools that already exist, for problems we know well. [Click] The engine already exists. Our work is the drivers – skilled people – and the roads – good data, good institutions, good training.
Interlude · ~2 min

A new way to talk to computers

From clicking, to coding, to simply saying what you need.

Before the three pillars, one short interlude. Something fundamental has changed in how people work with computers – and it matters a lot for TVET.

Click → Code → Prompt

Same task: “Which apprentices missed more than 3 days this month?”

Why this is empowering

Plain language becomes the interface. A trainer, a technician or a labour-market analyst can now describe what they need – without being a programmer.

But: you still have to know your trade to judge whether the answer is right.

Look at one everyday task in three ways. With clicks, you need to know where every menu is. With code, you need to learn a programming language. With a prompt, you just say what you need – in your own words, maybe even in your own language. [Click] That is empowering: the barrier to using a computer drops dramatically. But the knowledge of the trade becomes more important, not less – because someone has to judge whether the answer is right.

Offline AI or online AI – where does your data go?

Measured on this very keynote: offline draft 46 min · cloud draft 4 min · offline draft + cloud polish ≈ 64 % fewer cloud tokens. See the numbers →

Here is a choice every GIZ project will face. Offline AI means an open model running on a server you own – at a university, a ministry, a training centre. The data never leaves the building. Online AI means a commercial cloud service – usually stronger and faster, but your data travels to a data centre abroad. [Click] I tested both on this very keynote. The local server needed 46 minutes for a first draft; the cloud needed four. The best value came from mixing: draft offline, polish online. The details are on the "behind the scenes" page.

The AI never touches the computer

A language model only writes text – sometimes that text is code or a command. A program around it, with permissions you set, decides what is actually done.

Steps 1–4 are taken from the real log of the offline run that drafted this keynote.
This is the most important technical idea of the talk, and it is simple. The AI itself never touches a computer. A language model only produces text. When that text is a command – "read this file", "write that file" – a separate program, the agent, checks whether it is allowed and only then runs it on the computer. The result goes back to the model as new text. Let me click through how this keynote was drafted. [Click through] And notice the last step: when the model asks for something outside the rules, the program stops and asks a human. That is where control lives: in permissions, in sandboxes, in human approval – things we can teach, regulate and own.

Labour market information travels a path, from the first job ad to a decision in a ministry or a training centre. [Click] Here is step two in miniature: the computer reads a job ad and pulls out the skills. Edit the ad and try it yourself. A real example: the EU agency Cedefop does this for millions of online job ads; shared skill lists like ESCO make jobs and skills comparable. The big gain is freshness – what employers need now, not what the last census said.

Now a harder question: who owns all this data? [Click] Data sovereignty means ministries, public employment services and TVET authorities keep access to their data and own it. [Click] Job-seekers' data needs consent and protection; systems trained on past hiring can repeat old unfairness; the EU treats AI in hiring and education as high-risk – a useful reference for our partners. [Click] And there is a practical way to keep control: run openly available AI models on your own servers, as my university does.

Imagine a plumber in Nairobi. Skilled, always busy, trusted by the neighbourhood – but not on LinkedIn or any job portal. Pillar 2 · Training for AI-supported systems · ~4 min

How should TVET prepare technicians for machines that contain AI?

Second pillar. More and more technical systems in our partner countries contain AI: sensors plus software that spot problems and predict failures. How does TVET prepare the people who run and repair them?

The AI suggests. Grace decides.

Grace is a water technician. The leak-detection system sends her a work order. You are Grace – what do you do?

Same pattern elsewhere: a solar inverter “underperforming”, a camera that flags “faulty parts” on a production line. Illustrative cases.

Let us make it concrete. You are Grace, a water technician. Sensors in the pipes report something; the software suggests what it means. Only Grace can judge whether the alarm is real. Let us play two or three cases together. [Play] Notice: the AI was useful every time – but in some cases it was wrong, and the knowledge of the trade made the difference. The same pattern holds for solar inverters and quality cameras in production.

What changes for skilled workers

Skilled workers now work with the system:

  • Read its suggestions: does this make sense?
  • Notice when it is wrong
  • Feed it good data, look after the sensors
  • Report problems to the right person
LevelWhat it means
1 Useoperate AI-supported tools safely
2 Understand & checkknow what it can and cannot do, spot errors, protect data
3 Maintain & adaptkeep sensors accurate, update data, find and fix faults

Built into each trade – water technician, electrician, mechatronics technician. Not a separate IT course.

For Grace and her colleagues, the daily work changes: they work with the system. [Click] A simple model with three levels: use, understand and check, maintain and adapt. Every occupation needs its own mix. [Click] And these skills belong inside each occupation's training – not in a separate computer course. UNESCO's 2024 AI competency frameworks for students and teachers are a good reference. In practice: update occupational standards, train teachers and in-company trainers first, offer realistic equipment or simulations, and work with companies that already run such systems.

How can AI make learning personal, self-paced and open?

Third pillar. Now we turn the question around. Not training for AI – but how AI can help us train people.

Learning that fits the learner

Imagine Joseph, an apprentice electrician. During the day he works; in the evening he learns on his phone. An AI tutor can explain a topic again – in simpler words, in another language, with an example from his trade – and give him practice questions with instant feedback. [Click] Open Educational Resources are free, openly licensed materials; with AI, adapting them to a local language or trade becomes much faster. [Click] An example from my own work: an open-source Teaching Agent helps teachers turn their course plan into interactive online material. It drafts, checks and suggests – the teacher stays the author. In fact, this keynote was drafted with it.

Risks: confident wrong answers · copying instead of learning · unequal access · privacy of young learners

If teachers are not confident with AI, learners will not be either.
Imagine Sara, a teacher at a vocational school. AI can save her time – but it cannot do her job. Let us test ourselves: the tutor gives an answer and sounds very sure. What is wrong? [Let audience answer] The formula is wrong, but the number happens to be right – exactly the kind of mistake a learner will not notice unless they know the subject. [Click] Sara keeps what matters: goals, judging quality, motivation, fair assessment. Her learners need critical thinking above all. Let us name the risks honestly – none is a reason to stop, they are reasons to teach carefully. [Click] That is why I would start with teachers.
