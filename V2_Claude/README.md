# AI in TVET and Employment Promotion – Applying AI Sensibly

**Virtual keynote · GIZ TVET and Labour Market community**

Hannes Tegelbeckers  
Otto von Guericke University Magdeburg  
Engineering pedagogy and technical education

                --{{0}}--
Hello everyone, and welcome to this conference. In the next fifteen minutes I want to take some of the mystery out of AI. My question is simple: what should GIZ focus on when we talk about AI in TVET and employment promotion? My short answer: less on technology, more on people.


# Setting the scene

**Is AI something new – or is it already part of our working day?**

<!-- time: about 2 minutes for this part (3 slides) -->

                --{{0}}--
Let us start with where we are today. AI is not something that will arrive one day in the future. In many small ways, it is already here.


## Where did you meet AI this week?

Pick the one that fits best. There is no right or wrong answer.

- [(1)] Spell-check or translation on my phone
- [(2)] A chatbot that answered a question
- [(3)] A voice message that was turned into text
- [(4)] Route planning or a delivery app
- [(5)] I am not sure – maybe nowhere

{{1}}
<section>

**All of these are AI.** Recruitment platforms also use it to sort CVs.

**In plain words:** AI is software that has learned patterns from many examples. It uses them to suggest, predict, sort or write.

It does **not** understand like a person – and it can be **confidently wrong**.

</section>

                --{{0}}--
Please take a few seconds and vote in the poll. Where did you meet AI this week? Choose the answer that fits best.

                --{{1}}--
Thank you for voting. Most of us use AI every week, we just do not call it AI. By AI I simply mean software that has learned from many examples and now suggests, predicts, sorts or writes. It does not understand like a person does, and it can be wrong while sounding very sure.


## Apply, don't build

**Building** a large AI model from scratch costs huge amounts of money, data and energy. Only a handful of companies do it.

{{1}}
<section>

**Applying** existing tools to real problems – that is where we can make a difference:

- matching people to jobs
- making training more relevant
- supporting teachers

</section>

{{2}}
<section>

**We do not need to build the engine – we need to train good drivers and build good roads.**

</section>

                --{{0}}--
Here is the key point for development cooperation. A model is the trained core of an AI system, the part that has learned from examples. Building a big one from scratch is a race for a few very rich companies, and we do not need to join it.

                --{{1}}--
Our strength lies somewhere else: using existing tools well for real problems. Matching people to jobs. Making training more relevant. Supporting teachers.

                --{{2}}--
So here is the picture for the rest of this talk. We do not need to build the engine. We need to train good drivers and build good roads. I will show you three such roads.


# Pillar 1 – Data for labour-market analysis

**Who is visible in our labour-market data – and who is not?**

<!-- time: about 3 minutes for this part (4 slides) -->

                --{{0}}--
The first road is data. Good decisions about jobs and training need good information about the labour market. So let us ask: who is visible in that information, and who is missing?


## From job ads to better decisions

```ascii
  collect    -->  clean & link    -->  analyse     -->  share & use
  job ads         remove doubles       trends           career guidance
  surveys         match to a           skill gaps       curriculum updates
  registers       skills list                           policy
```

{{1}}
<section>

**What AI can add**

- Read thousands of online job ads per day and pull out job titles and skills.
- Show what employers ask for **this month** – not only in the last census.
- Suggest jobs to job-seekers, and training offers to people with a skill gap.

</section>

{{2}}
<section>

**Real example:** the EU agency Cedefop analyses millions of online job ads to track skill demand in Europe (Skills-OVATE). Shared skill lists such as the European ESCO classification help to compare jobs and skills.

</section>

                --{{0}}--
Labour-market information moves along a simple chain. We collect it, we clean and link it, we analyse it, and then people use it: for career guidance, for new curricula, for policy.

                --{{1}}--
AI helps most at the beginning of this chain. It can read thousands of job ads every day and list the skills employers ask for. That gives us a picture of this month, not only of the last census. It can also suggest jobs to job-seekers and training to people with a skill gap.

                --{{2}}--
This is not science fiction. In Europe, the EU agency Cedefop already does this with millions of online job ads. A shared list of skills, like the European ESCO classification, makes jobs and skills comparable.


## Whose data is it?

Job-portal data often sits with private platforms abroad.

{{1}}
<section>

**Data sovereignty** – in plain words: our partners keep access to their data and own it. Partners are ministries, public employment services and TVET authorities.

</section>

{{2}}
<section>

| Concern | What helps |
| --- | --- |
| Personal data of job-seekers | Consent, use only for the agreed purpose, good security |
| Bias: a system that learned from past hiring can repeat past discrimination, e.g. against women or minorities | Extra safeguards. The EU AI Act (2024) counts AI for recruitment, for access to vocational training and for assessing learners as **high-risk** – a useful reference point |
| Data leaving the country | Run open AI models (free to download) on local servers, so the data never leaves the country or institution. Example: my university runs a desktop-sized AI server for teaching material |

</section>

                --{{0}}--
Now a question we should always ask: who owns the data? Often the data from job portals sits with private companies in other countries.

                --{{1}}--
Data sovereignty simply means that our partners keep control. The ministry, the employment service and the TVET authority should have access to the data and own it.

                --{{2}}--
Three concerns need care. Personal data needs consent and good protection. A system that learned from past hiring can repeat past unfairness, which is why the EU AI Act treats AI in recruitment and training as high-risk. And data can stay at home: open AI models, which are free to download, can run on a local server, as we do at my university.


## The blind spot: the informal sector

Imagine a plumber in Nairobi. She is skilled and has work – but no LinkedIn profile, and she never appears in a job ad.

{{1}}
<section>

ILO estimate: about **2 billion workers** – roughly **6 in 10** worldwide – are in informal employment. In Africa the share is around **85 %**.
<!-- PRÜFEN: latest ILO figures (about 2 billion, roughly 6 in 10 worldwide, around 85 % in Africa) -->

Online job ads show only the formal, urban, digital part of the labour market.

</section>

{{2}}
<section>

**Ways to make them visible**

- short phone or SMS surveys
- voice-based skill profiles in local languages
- data from informal apprenticeship and trade associations
- records from recognition of prior learning
- mobile-money or market data – with consent

**If it is not in the data, it is not in the decision.**

</section>

                --{{0}}--
Now imagine a plumber in Nairobi. She is good at her job and she has customers. But she has no LinkedIn profile and no job portal knows about her.

                --{{1}}--
She is not an exception. According to the ILO, most workers in many partner countries work informally. Job ads only show the formal, urban and digital part of the labour market.

                --{{2}}--
If it is not in the data, it is not in the decision. AI will make this gap bigger unless we collect data from the informal sector on purpose. Short SMS surveys, voice profiles in local languages, or records from trade associations are good places to start.


# Pillar 2 – Training for AI-augmented systems

**Who will keep the smart machines running – and who checks them?**

<!-- time: about 4 minutes for this part (4 slides) -->

                --{{0}}--
The second road is about skilled workers. More and more machines and technical systems have AI built in. Someone has to run them, check them and repair them.


## AI is moving into technical systems

Sensors measure, software spots unusual readings, predicts failures and fine-tunes how the system runs.

{{1}}
<section>

| Example (illustrative) | What the AI does | What the skilled worker does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Smart water | Sensors in pipes detect leaks; software predicts where a pipe will fail | Gets a work order on the phone – and judges if the alarm is real |
| Solar energy | Monitoring software flags a solar inverter that underperforms | Checks, cleans or replaces parts |
| Automated production | A camera with image recognition spots faulty parts | Adjusts settings; keeps camera and lighting set up correctly |

</section>

                --{{0}}--
In partner countries, technical systems increasingly contain AI. Sensors collect measurements, and software looks for anything unusual, predicts failures and adjusts the system.

                --{{1}}--
Here are three examples. A water technician gets a leak alarm on the phone and must judge if it is real. A solar technician checks the inverter, the box that turns solar power into usable electricity. And in a factory, staff keep the camera that spots faulty parts working well.


## Working with the system

Skilled workers read the system's suggestions, check if they make sense, notice when it is wrong, feed it good data, look after the sensors, write down what happened and pass problems on when needed.

{{1}}
<section>

1. **Use** – operate AI-supported tools safely
2. **Understand and check** – know what the system can and cannot do, spot errors, protect data
3. **Maintain and adapt** – adjust sensors so they measure correctly, update data, find and fix faults, change settings

</section>

{{2}}
<section>

**Job-specific, not a separate IT course:** AI skills belong inside each occupation – water technician, electrician, mechatronics technician.

<small>Reference: UNESCO AI competency frameworks for students and for teachers (2024).</small>

</section>

                --{{0}}--
What changes for the skilled worker? They do not just press buttons. They work with the system, check its suggestions and notice when it is wrong.

                --{{1}}--
I find three levels helpful. First, use the tools safely. Second, understand and check: what can the system do, and where does it make mistakes? Third, maintain and adapt: adjust, update, repair, set up.

                --{{2}}--
This goes beyond basic IT skills. AI skills must be part of each occupation's training, not an extra computer course. UNESCO's AI competency frameworks from 2024 point the same way, with a human-centred mindset and ethics at their core.


## What TVET systems need to do

- Update occupational standards and curricula
- Train TVET teachers and in-company trainers **first**
- Provide realistic training equipment or simulations
- Cooperate with companies that already run such systems

                --{{0}}--
So what does this mean for TVET systems? Occupational standards and curricula need updating. Teachers and in-company trainers must learn first, because they cannot teach what they have never used. And learners need realistic equipment or simulations, ideally in cooperation with companies that already run such systems.


# Pillar 3 – Training humans with AI

**How can AI help people learn – without replacing the teacher?**

<!-- time: about 3 minutes for this part (3 slides) -->

                --{{0}}--
The third road turns the question around. Not training people for AI, but training people with AI. How can it help people learn, while the teacher stays in charge?


## A tutor in every pocket?

Imagine a learner from a vocational school, studying on her phone in the evening.

{{1}}
<section>

- **Personal:** an AI tutor explains a topic again – in simpler words, in another language, with another example.
- **Practice:** it creates practice questions and gives instant feedback.
- **Self-paced and interactive:** quizzes, simulations, step-by-step tasks.

</section>

{{2}}
<section>

**Open Educational Resources (OER)** are free, openly licensed materials that anyone may reuse and adapt. AI makes it much faster to adapt them to a local context, language or occupation. UNESCO adopted a Recommendation on OER in 2019.

</section>

                --{{0}}--
Imagine a learner who studies on her phone in the evening, after work. She does not understand a topic the first time.

                --{{1}}--
An AI tutor can explain it again, in simpler words, in her own language, with a different example. It can give her practice questions and tell her at once if she is right. She learns at her own pace.

                --{{2}}--
Open Educational Resources make this stronger. These are free materials that anyone may use and adapt. With AI, adapting them to a local language or occupation becomes much faster.


## Co-pilot, not autopilot

| Teachers keep | Learners need |
| --- | --- |
| setting goals | critical thinking: "Is this answer correct? How do I check?" |
| judging quality | problem solving and communication |
| relationship and motivation | teamwork and responsibility |
| assessing fairly | |

{{1}}
<section>

**Watch out for:** wrong answers that sound confident · copying instead of learning · unequal access · data protection of minors

</section>

{{2}}
<section>

**Example from my work:** an open-source "Teaching Agent" helps teachers turn a course plan into interactive online material (LiaScript, an open format that runs in any browser). The teacher stays the author and decides – the agent drafts, checks and suggests.

**Teachers first:** if TVET teachers are not confident with AI, learners will not be either.

</section>

<!-- PRÜFEN: decide whether to mention the Teaching Agent example and the fact that this keynote was drafted with it in three ways (offline on a local university server, with a commercial AI assistant, and offline first plus human-guided improvement) -->

                --{{0}}--
AI should be a co-pilot, not an autopilot. Teachers still set goals, judge quality, motivate and assess fairly. Learners need what AI cannot replace: critical thinking, problem solving, teamwork and responsibility.

                --{{1}}--
We should also be honest about the risks. AI can give wrong answers that sound very sure. Learners may copy instead of learn. Not everyone has the same access, and the data of young learners needs special protection.

                --{{2}}--
In my own work, an open-source Teaching Agent helps teachers build interactive online courses, but the teacher stays the author and decides. And one rule matters most: if TVET teachers are not confident with AI, their learners will not be either.


# Conclusion – Where should GIZ invest?

**Where should our projects put their money and energy?**

<!-- time: about 2 minutes for this part (3 slides) -->

                --{{0}}--
Let me bring the three roads together. Where should GIZ projects invest, so that AI really helps people in TVET and employment?


## Where should your project start?

Five places to invest. Pick the **one** your project needs most right now.

- [(1)] **Low-barrier and mobile-first** – simple smartphones, messaging apps or SMS, voice and local languages, weak internet
- [(2)] **Capacity building** – teachers, trainers, labour-market analysts, employment services: people before platforms
- [(3)] **Local ownership** – partners own their data, run tools locally where possible, prefer open-source tools and open formats
- [(4)] **Include the informal sector** – in data and in training offers
- [(5)] **Start small, learn fast** – pilots with clear problems, scale what works, share across projects

                --{{0}}--
Here are five places to invest, and people always come before platforms. Solutions should work on simple phones and with weak internet. Partners should own their data and prefer open-source tools, which anyone may use and adapt, so they are not stuck with one provider. Please vote now: where should your own project start?


## Three take-aways

1. **Apply, don't build** – AI is a tool for real problems.
2. **People first** – skilled workers and teachers who can work with AI and question it.
3. **Data with care** – local ownership, privacy, and the informal sector in the picture.

# Behind the scenes: one keynote, built three ways

**Same brief, same fact sheet, same open-source Teaching Agent – three ways of running it.**

                --{{0}}--
This keynote practises what it preaches. I built it with an open-source Teaching Agent, in three ways: offline on our own university server, with a commercial cloud AI, and offline first with the cloud AI improving the draft. Here is what that cost and what it gave.


## Three routes

```ascii
               brief + fact sheet + Teaching Agent  (same for all three)
                                  |
        +-------------------------+-------------------------+
        |                         |                         |
   V1 OFFLINE                V2 CLOUD ONLY            V3 OFFLINE + CLOUD
   local open model          Claude Opus 5.5          V1 draft, then Claude
   on the university         builds the whole         improves it (one
   AI server (GX10)          keynote                  editing session)
        |                         |                         |
   46 min, no cloud           4 min, all in            46 min offline
   tokens, data stays         the cloud                + 3 min cloud
   on campus
```

                --{{0}}--
All three started from the same written brief and the same fact sheet. Only the engine changed: a free open model on a desktop-sized server at our university, a commercial cloud model, or both one after the other.


## Token usage and effort (measured)

| | V1 Offline (GX10) | V2 Cloud only (Claude) | V3 Offline + Claude improve |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Model | qwen3.8:27b, local | Claude Opus 5.5 | qwen3.8:27b → Claude Opus 5.5 |
| Runtime | 46 min (23 steps) | 4.2 min (38 turns) | 46 min + 2.9 min (12 turns) |
| Local model tokens (in / out) | 947 k / 35 k | – | 947 k / 35 k |
| Claude fresh input + cache write | 0 | 162 k | 41 k |
| Claude cache reads | 0 | 1.19 M | 0.32 M |
| Claude output (incl. thinking) | 0 | 35.8 k (13.9 k) | 18.5 k (6.9 k) |
| **Claude input-token equivalents¹** | **0** | **≈ 459 k** | **≈ 165 k (–64 %)** |
| Claude list price | 0 € | ≈ 2.25 USD | ≈ 0.76 USD |
| Syntax errors (lint) | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| Slides | 17 | 18 | 18 |

<small>¹ Weighting as in the GX10 wiki: fresh input/cache write × 1, cache read × 0.1, output × 5. Not included and the same for all three: preparing brief and fact sheet (≈ 150 k equivalents, estimated) and the human review before the talk.</small>

                --{{0}}--
The numbers are measured from the logs. The offline run used almost a million tokens of model work, but none of them cost cloud tokens. Improving the offline draft took about a third of the cloud effort of writing the keynote directly with the cloud model.


## Pros and cons

| | Pros | Cons |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **V1 Offline** | no cloud cost · data never leaves the university · clean syntax · follows the brief and the fact sheet faithfully | slow (46 min) · reads like the fact sheet, copied almost word for word · jargon kept · crowded slides · small claims nobody asked for · its own self-check said "all fine" and gave a wrong date |
| **V2 Cloud only** | best keynote straight away · plain words and clear tables · self-review caught jargon · ready in 4 min | highest cloud cost · all material goes to a cloud provider · still needs the speaker's review |
| **V3 Offline + improve** | quality on the level of V2, with a thread of people (Amina, Grace, Joseph, Sara) and a concrete call to action · ≈ 64 % fewer cloud tokens than V2 · offline part can run overnight | two steps, ≈ 50 min in total · only about 55 % of the offline draft survived · the draft still goes to the cloud for improvement |

                --{{0}}--
The pattern is the same one we saw for skilled workers. The local model does the heavy, routine part. A stronger system and finally a human check, shorten and make it human. None of the three versions was ready without that last human look.


## What this means for GIZ projects

- **Local open models are good enough for first drafts** – and keep sensitive data in the country.
- **Quality comes from the improvement step** – by a stronger model *and* by people.
- **Mix and match:** offline for volume and privacy, cloud for polish – it costs about a third.
- **Never trust the AI's own quality report** – check it yourself.

                --{{0}}--
For partner institutions this is encouraging. A small local AI server can do much of the routine work at no running cost, and data stays at home. The polish, and the responsibility, stay with people.
